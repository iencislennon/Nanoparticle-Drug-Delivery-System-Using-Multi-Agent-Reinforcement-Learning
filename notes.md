The endothelium is a single-layered sheet of flat cells of mesenchymal origin lining the inner surface of blood and lymphatic vessels and the cavities of the heart. This structure, weighing 1.5–2 kg, acts as an active endocrine organ in humans, synthesizing substances to control blood clotting, vascular tone, blood pressure, and renal filtration. The endothelium regulates the diffusion of substances, responds to mechanical and chemical stress, and prevents thrombosis and inflammation.

Tight junctions are sealed connections between endothelial cells in normal vessels, particularly in brain capillaries and other barrier tissues, that limit permeability to macromolecules and cells. Leaky vessels are characterized by weakening or disruption of these connections, leading to increased wall permeability, as seen in inflammation, ischemia-reperfusion injury, or tumors. In healthy tissues, tight junctions provide barrier function, while leaky vessels allow the leakage of plasma, leukocytes, and other substances, contributing to pathologies.

The EPR effect (enhanced permeability and retention) occurs in tumor tissues due to leaky vessels with defective tight junctions and a lack of lymphatic drainage. This allows nanoparticles (10–200 nm in diameter) to penetrate the tumor interstitium and accumulate there passively, unlike in normal tissues with tight junctions. The essence of the effect is the selective delivery of drugs via nanomedicine for cancer therapy, although its effectiveness varies due to vascular heterogeneity.

The extracellular matrix (ECM) consists of a complex of proteins and polysaccharides that provide structure and support to tissues. The main components of the ECM are collagens (especially more than 18 types), glycoproteins (e.g., fibronectin, laminin, elastin), proteoglycans, and glycosaminoglycans, the most common of which is hyaluronic acid.

The ECM prevents nanoparticle penetration due to its dense mesh structure formed by collagen and other components, which limits particle diffusion and movement. Collagen, in particular, acts as a major barrier, reducing nanoparticle mobility.

Collagen density is a measure of collagen concentration in the ECM. It is characterized by the density of the collagen fiber network, which can be measured, for example, by physicochemical methods. Higher collagen density indicates a denser and more compact structural network, which limits the mobility of cells and nanoparticles.

Endocytosis is the active process of taking in external material by a cell by retracting a portion of the plasma membrane and forming intracellular membrane vesicles such as endosomes.

Nanoparticles enter cells primarily through endocytosis: the membrane envelops them, budding off an endocytic vesicle, which is used in nanomedicines to deliver substances. This mechanism depends on the size, coating, and type of nanoparticles, as well as the cell type, ensuring targeted transport.
